# How to use the Systems Card Game

The Systems Card Game is designed to be adapted to the system, participants, and purpose of a particular workshop or course. Below, you can find basic instructions on how to adapt the Systems Card Game to your own ideas an purposes, as well as links to material and further guidance.

**Before you start making your own adaptation, check out existing ones you might be able to use [here](applications/)**

The Systems Card Game can be used in two connected ways. You can **adapt the cards** to represent a particular system, livelihood or topic, and you can **use the cards in a workshop** to support research, dialogue, learning or collaborative planning.

You do not have to follow every step described on this page. Start with the purpose of your activity and select the parts of the method that help you achieve it.

## Adapt the cards {#adapt-the-cards}

You can use an existing card set or adapt the Systems Card Game to a particular system, livelihood, community or topic.

Adapting the cards means deciding:

- which elements of the system should appear on the cards;
- which categories will help participants approach the system;
- whether the cards will be prepared before the workshop or developed with participants during the workshop.

The card set does not need to provide a complete or final description of the system. It provides a starting point for discussion, mapping and learning.

### Systems and system maps {#systems-and-mapping}

A system consists of elements that interact with one another. These may include people, organisations, resources, activities, environmental conditions, rules, infrastructure and many other factors.

The relationships between these elements influence how the system behaves. A change in one part of the system may affect several other parts, sometimes through indirect connections or feedbacks.

A system map is a simplified representation of this complexity. It does not provide a single, objective picture of the system. Instead, it makes participants’ knowledge and assumptions visible so that they can examine relationships, compare perspectives and identify questions together.

Before developing a card set, it therefore helps to clarify:

- What system or topic are you interested in?
- What question should the cards help people explore?
- Where will you draw the boundaries of the system?
- Whose knowledge and perspective should inform the cards?

### Decide when to identify the system elements {#identify-system-elements}

There are three ways to develop the content of the card set.

| Approach | How it works | Particularly useful when |
|---|---|---|
| **Prepare the cards before the workshop** | Organisers collect information about the system, identify relevant elements and produce a ready-made deck. | The system is already reasonably well understood, workshop time is limited, or several groups should work with comparable cards. |
| **Develop the elements during the workshop** | Participants identify important system elements themselves, using blank cards or another collection process. | The purpose is exploratory, participants’ knowledge is central, or the system is being approached from scratch. |
| **Combine both approaches** | Organisers provide a prepared deck, while participants can add missing elements, question existing cards or suggest different terminology. | The workshop needs some structure but should remain open to participants’ knowledge and perspectives. |

The choice should follow the purpose of the workshop. See [Plan a workshop](#plan-a-workshop) for guidance on selecting an appropriate workshop format.

### Identify potential system elements {#select-system-elements}

If you prepare the cards in advance, begin by gathering information about the system. Depending on your purpose, this might include:

- existing research, reports or other documents;
- interviews or discussions with people familiar with the system;
- observations and practical experience;
- survey material or other structured data;
- results from previous workshops or mapping activities.

Look for elements that help describe how the system works and what influences it. These may include actors, organisations, resources, places, activities, environmental conditions, technologies, rules, values or sources of change.

Use short, recognisable terms on the cards. Participants should be able to interpret a card and discuss what it means in the context of their system. If a term could have several meanings, you can clarify it in accompanying guidance or during facilitation.

Do not try to include everything. A very large deck can make it difficult for participants to recognise priorities and relationships. Focus on elements that are relevant to the purpose of the activity, and provide blank cards so participants can add what is missing.

### Develop the card categories {#card-categories}

Categories help people approach a complex system from several perspectives. They can also help the developers of a card set notice gaps or an excessive focus on one part of the system.

The general Systems Card Game concept uses four broad categories:

1. **External conditions:** The wider conditions that shape the system from outside the actors’ immediate control.
2. **Governance and society:** The rules, institutions and social structures that influence how the system functions.
3. **Practices and activities:** The actions, routines and ways of working through which the system operates.
4. **Resource availability and competition:** The resources, access and space on which the system depends, including competing demands for them.

These categories provide a starting point rather than a fixed classification. For a particular application, you can:

- rename a category so that it makes sense in the context;
- divide one broad category into two or more specific categories;
- combine categories that do not need to remain separate;
- introduce additional categories;
- leave out a category that is not relevant.

The categories do not have to be mutually exclusive. The same system element may be relevant in more than one category. In that case, you can place it where it is most useful, discuss its overlap with participants, or include versions of the card in more than one category.

### Use colours and icons together {#colours-and-icons}

Categories can be distinguished through colour, icons or both.

The icons in the general template are suggestions. You can replace them with symbols that are more meaningful for the system or community you are working with.

Using both an icon and a colour makes the categories easier to recognise:

- when cards are printed in greyscale;
- for participants who do not distinguish all colours easily;
- when similar or identical system elements appear in different categories.

Do not rely on colour alone to communicate a card’s category. Keep the meaning of each icon consistent throughout the deck and explain the categories at the beginning of the workshop.

### Review the proposed set {#review-card-set}

Before producing the final cards, review the proposed set with people who understand the system or will help facilitate the workshop.

Check whether:

- the card set reflects the purpose of the workshop;
- important parts of the system are missing;
- one category contains far more cards than the others;
- the category names make sense to the intended participants;
- the wording on the cards is clear and respectful;
- specialist terminology needs explanation or translation;
- the cards represent more than one perspective on the system;
- blank cards will be available for additional elements.

If possible, test the cards in a small pilot session. Participants’ questions often reveal unclear wording, missing elements or categories that need adjustment.

### Make and print the cards {#make-the-cards}

Once you have selected the system elements, categories, wording, colours and icons, you can produce the physical card set.

The general template is designed for printing on A4 sticker paper. The printed cards can be cut out and attached to an ordinary deck of playing cards.

Before printing a complete set, print one test sheet. Check the page size, scaling, colours, legibility and cutting marks.

Keep some blank cards available during the workshop so participants can add missing elements.

### The card set can continue to develop

An adapted card set does not have to remain unchanged. Workshop participants may identify missing elements, use different terminology or question the categories chosen by the developers.

Record these observations after each workshop. They can help you revise the deck or create a new version for a different purpose or participant group.

Once your cards are ready, continue to [Plan a workshop](#plan-a-workshop).

## Plan a workshop {#plan-a-workshop}

There is no single correct format for a Systems Card Game workshop. The activities you choose should depend on **why you are bringing people together** and what participants should gain from the process.

The cards can support:

- **Research:** gathering knowledge about how participants understand a system, its relationships, pressures and possible futures;
- **Communication and outreach:** helping communities, stakeholders and researchers share perspectives and understand one another’s knowledge and priorities;
- **Education:** introducing systems thinking and exploring how environmental, social, economic and political factors interact;
- **Planning and reflection:** identifying vulnerable connections, possible disruptions, ways of responding and conditions needed to reach a desired future.

A workshop does not always have to produce a complete system map. For an introductory, educational or outreach activity, it may be enough to select important cards, discuss what they represent and consider which categories of elements influence the system.

Building a map becomes particularly useful when participants want to explore relationships, identify critical connections or consider how pressures and unexpected developments might affect the system.

### Choose the purpose of the workshop {#workshop-purpose}

A workshop can include one or more of the following stages.

#### 1. Explore the elements of the system

Participants select cards that they consider important and discuss what those elements mean. Blank cards can be used to add missing elements.

This can be a useful activity on its own, particularly when the aim is learning, starting a conversation or comparing different perspectives.

#### 2. Build a shared system map

Participants arrange the cards and draw connections between them. They can discuss which elements are central, which relationships are particularly important and where participants understand the system differently.

A system map makes relationships visible and provides a shared basis for deeper discussion.

#### 3. Explore pressures, surprises and preparedness

Participants introduce an external pressure, disruptive development or “surprise” and consider how it might affect the map.

Questions might include:

- Which elements or connections would be affected first?
- Where might effects spread through the system?
- Which connections could weaken or break?
- What existing strengths would help the system respond?
- What could people do to prepare or adapt?

This stage is particularly useful when the workshop aims to identify vulnerabilities, critical connections or possible courses of action.

#### 4. Explore desired futures

Instead of beginning with threats, participants can begin with their hopes, aspirations or “dreams” for the system.

They can then ask:

- What would a desirable future look like?
- What needs to happen within the system for that future to become possible?
- Which elements or relationships are most important?
- Who has the ability or responsibility to bring about change?
- Can different desired futures coexist?
- How might conflicts between them be addressed?

This is the basis of the **Surprises and Dreams** approach. It connects systems mapping with positive, future-oriented discussion.

### Group size and scaling {#group-size}

The method is scalable. An ideal discussion group consists of approximately **five or six participants**, supported by **one or two facilitators**.

One facilitator can chair the discussion, explain the activities and ensure that everyone has an opportunity to contribute. A second facilitator can take notes and help document the map and discussion.

A workshop can include several groups working at the same time, provided that there are:

- enough copies of the card set;
- separate tables and sufficient working space;
- large sheets of paper or another suitable mapping surface;
- enough facilitators for all groups;
- sufficient time to bring the groups together and compare their observations.

If several groups take part, their maps do not have to be identical. Comparing the maps can reveal different experiences, priorities and ways of understanding the same system.

### Prepare the workshop {#prepare-the-workshop}

Before the workshop, decide:

- what the workshop is intended to achieve;
- which stages of the method are needed;
- who should participate;
- how much time is available;
- who will facilitate and take notes;
- whether the discussion, maps or participants will be photographed or recorded;
- how workshop materials and results will be used and shared.

Prepare one card set for each group, including blank cards for missing elements. Depending on the planned activities, groups may also need large sheets of paper, pens, markers in different colours, stickers and materials for recording observations.

If the workshop forms part of research, participants should receive clear information about consent, documentation, anonymity, data storage and how the results will be used. Photographs showing identifiable participants should only be taken and published with appropriate permission.

### Close and follow up {#workshop-follow-up}

At the end of the workshop:

- invite participants to identify an insight they gained;
- ask for feedback on the activity and facilitation;
- explain what will happen to the maps, notes and photographs;
- clarify whether participants will receive a report or other follow-up;
- discuss possible next steps where appropriate.

Soon after the workshop, facilitators should compile their notes and photographs while the discussion is still fresh. Participants should receive any information or results promised during the workshop.
